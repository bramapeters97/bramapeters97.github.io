MyPremiere+ | A personal cinema, across three screens

A family video library, designed for a moment on your phone, a closer look on your iPad, and an evening together in front of the television.

Explore the three demos ↗ Interactive browser studies · Fictional catalogue

From stored videos to something to watch

A video can be easy to keep and surprisingly difficult to return to. A folder tells you where a file lives; a good viewing experience helps you remember why you wanted to watch it.

MyPremiere+ explores that difference. I wanted to bring the familiarity of a streaming library to a personal collection: a welcoming home screen, recognisable artwork, a little context, and a clear path into playback. The collection belongs at the centre of the experience.

The idea develops the family-archive theme behind LELIBRAMBAS+ into a broader product design study. These three prototypes ask how that experience changes when the screen is in your hand, on a table, or across the living room.

The starting pointMake a collection inviting.

Give each title enough identity and context to encourage someone to press Play.

The design questionWhat should change with the screen?

Keep the library familiar while adapting the way someone reaches and moves through it.

A shared visual language

I used a deep navy foundation, warm gold accents and large landscape artwork to give the collection a quiet cinema feel. Navigation sits on a separate visual layer, while title information stays close to the artwork it describes.

Home offers a starting point; the library makes the small catalogue easy to scan; search supports a more deliberate visit. Title details connect the browse and playback experiences. Saving a film gives someone a simple way to come back to it.

Those ideas stay consistent across the prototypes. The layout, density and interaction change with each viewing context.

01 · iPhone

A small screen, with a clear next step

On the phone, I kept the interface focused on one decision at a time. A featured title introduces the collection; a compact two-column grid offers a quick browse; labelled bottom navigation keeps the main destinations close to the thumb.

Opening a title gives its description and viewing actions their own space. Back returns to the collection, so browsing feels continuous. Search and saved titles remain useful entry points without crowding the home screen.

02 · iPad

More room to explore

The tablet gives the library room to breathe. I moved the main destinations into a stable sidebar and let artwork and title rows occupy the wider canvas. More of the collection can be visible while the route through it remains easy to understand.

The important adaptation is how the extra space is used. Portrait and landscape layouts need to preserve readable titles, comfortable controls and a clear relationship between navigation and content. Simply enlarging the phone layout would leave much of that space without a useful purpose.

03 · Apple TV

Designed to be seen from the sofa

The television study gives artwork and typography more space, with a wide featured title and film rows that can be explored directionally. The viewing distance makes hierarchy especially important: the selected item needs to be obvious before anyone presses a button.

I treated focus and selection as separate actions. Moving to a title highlights it; selecting it opens its details. Back should return to a predictable place. In the browser demo, arrow keys, Enter and Escape stand in for those remote interactions, while a pointer remains available.

A deliberately small public experience

These demos open directly into one fictional viewing account. You can browse, search, save a title, read its details and try playback. The catalogue, names and artwork demonstrate the interface; they do not reveal a personal family archive. Every title plays the same short, silent illustrated sample, so you can explore the player without sharing personal footage.

I kept this public version centred on watching. Account creation, uploads and archive management are outside its scope. The demos run as static browser pages, separate from the full application’s services and databases. Saved titles and viewing progress, where used, stay in this browser and are not synchronised between the three demos.

These are interactive design prototypes for iPhone, iPad and Apple TV viewing contexts. Their browser behavior does not establish native accessibility, device integration or playback performance on Apple hardware.

What the three studies make clear

Working through the three layouts made the relationship between content and navigation more concrete. The phone needs economy, the tablet benefits from a stable overview, and the television depends on clear focus and a readable hierarchy at a distance.

The shared visual language gives the collection continuity. Adapting the controls gives each screen its own rhythm. That balance is the main outcome of this study: the same personal cinema can feel familiar without asking every device to behave the same way.

The current result is a set of browser prototypes for exploring those choices. Native implementation, hardware testing and the full product’s operational requirements remain separate work.
